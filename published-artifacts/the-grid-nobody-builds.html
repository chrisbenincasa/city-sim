Borough · procedural generation research · part two

The Grid Nobody Builds

Six agents went looking for how the field generates the city between the buildings — streets, blocks, parcels, ground. The sharpest thing they came back with is a number. Borough's lattice scores φ = 1.000 on a published measure of street-network order. Across one hundred world cities, the maximum is 0.899. The grain is right. The uniformity is off the end of the distribution.

00

First, a correction that reframes everything below

The research briefs — including the ones written for this batch — assumed Borough generates its road network at world creation. It does not.

Corrects the brief adr/0090 is titled the generator makes land and the player makes every road. The generator produces terrain, Woodland, hazard regions and Outside Connections with short road stubs. That is all. RoadGenerator.LayInto has exactly one call site — SyntheticCity, reached only through CommandKind.Populate, whose own comment in Simulation.cs reads: "Spike S0's verb, and the only one here that is an instrument rather than a player's."

So the uniform 32-tile lattice is a test fixture, not the shipped city's network. Everything measured on this page is measured against an instrument. That does not make the measurements useless — it is the world every screenshot, every balance run and every golden fixture is taken in, so it is the city the project actually looks at. But the design question is not "should Borough generate a better grid." It is what the player's network will need in order to be a network at all.

And the half that is worse — verified in Main.cs

The design says the player lays every Segment. The shell issues CommandKind.Populate at Tick 0 unconditionally — line 897, no flag, no branch, no path around it. There is no way to reach an empty world.

So every Borough city any human has ever looked at is a declared synthetic city on a 32-tile checkerboard, and the road verb has never been watched doing its job. The design says one thing and the only reachable artefact says another. Set against the amnesty's own amended Definition of done — a milestone is done when you have watched it happen and something surprised you — a checkerboard cannot surprise anybody.

The real gap, and it is not regularity

Borough has a three-way RoadKind — Street, Arterial, FootPath — with distinct speeds and capacities already wired: 50 against 90 km/h, 3,600 against 12,000 vehicles per hour. The BPR volume-delay loop in congested.toml would price them differently today.

But adr/0077 refuses Arterials in CommandKind.Connect — the player lays Streets only — and adr/0164 refuses them in the generator. Hierarchy exists in the data model and is reachable by neither the generator nor the player. It has no producer at all. That is the finding, and it is a smaller and more fixable thing than "the grid looks fake."

01

The measure, and what it says

Boeing (Applied Network Science 4:67, 2019) binned street bearings for one hundred world cities from OpenStreetMap and computed Shannon entropy. The instrument was built and it refuted the row it was built to confirm

Every Borough figure on this table was arithmetic until this morning: a perfect square lattice has φ = 1, 100% four-way and circuity 1 by definition, derivable at a desk without running anything. The measurement disagrees with all three. φ is 0.9987, not 1.000. Four-way is 84.0%, not 100%. Circuity is 0.9999, not 1.000. And the bearings occupy six compass bins rather than four — there are eight diagonal Segments in the 4,000-Citizen world that a pure lattice cannot contain.

The headline survives and one of its three legs does not. 0.9987 is still far past Chicago's 0.899, so no real city is anywhere near this ordered holds. But “100% four-way against a real maximum of 58%” was never true of the thing that runs.

And most of the shortfall is the perimeter rather than the shape. A small lattice is mostly edge, and an edge Node has degree 3. Measured on the same Ruleset at three sizes: four-way is 64.29% at 4,000 Citizens, 76.04% at 16,000, 84.03% at 64,000, converging on the ideal as the world grows. φ climbs the same way — 0.9974, 0.9983, 0.9987. So the honest reading of the gap is this city is small, not this city is varied, and quoting the 4,000-Citizen figure alone would argue the opposite of what is true.

φ does not notice an Arterial skeleton — which is a problem for move 3

rulesets/bordered.toml is the one shipped file that sets arterial_count = 16, and its curving Arterials occupy all 36 compass bins where the pure lattice occupies six. That is real orientation diversity, exactly the intervention section 07's move 3 recommends. φ moves from 0.9983 to 0.9982.

Half a million lattice Streets drown a handful of Arterials in the distribution. So an Arterial skeleton is not defensible on this measure — whatever else recommends it, it does not make the network measurably less ordered. Whether that is a true property of the index or an artefact of an implementation whose formula has not been checked against Boeing's paper is unresolved, and the code says so at its definition.

Three readings

No city reaches 1.0. The ceiling is Chicago at 0.899 — a city whose own ordinances specified eight streets to the mile one way and sixteen the other. Even it is missing a tenth of its order.

No city exceeds about 58% four-way junctions. Buenos Aires at 57.6% and Manhattan at 57.2% are the maxima across a hundred cities. Borough measures 84.0% at 64,000 Citizens and rising — well past the real ceiling, and not the 100% this page previously claimed. This is still the most discriminating statistic on the table; it is now a measured one.

Barcelona is 0.108 despite the Eixample, because Ciutat Vella and Gràcia are in the same city. A district-scale perfect grid coexisting with non-grid fabric is what "grid cities" actually are.

The narrowing result

The grain is right and the uniformity is not. 128 m blocks give roughly 61 intersections per square kilometre; Boeing's population-weighted mean for Northern America is 53.2, and Borough's median segment of 128 m sits beside Chicago's 105 and a North American mean of 100. Do not change block_tiles. Every measure of size is inside the real range; every measure of variation is off the end of it.

02

Why a flat network reads as not-a-city

The argument is empirical rather than aesthetic. Serra & Hillier tested the dual-network model against the whole UK road network and 20,752 traffic count points:

a foreground network of main paths, made up of a small number of long lines joined at wide obtuse angles, forming a web of highly central, multi-directional alignments; embedded in a background network of local streets, made up of a much larger number of shorter lines, forming grid-like patterns with lower centrality. Serra & Hillier, Computers, Environment and Urban Systems, 2018

And the sentence that makes it a finding rather than a description: the two-tier movement signature is "entirely absent in rural areas." It is a property of cities specifically. A network with no foreground does not read as a small city — it reads as not a city.

Kirkley et al. (Nature Communications 9:2501, 2018) found the same shape from the other direction across 97 cities: betweenness centrality is bimodal, "an underlying tree structure for high betweenness nodes, and a low betweenness regime corresponding to loops." Real networks carry a tree-shaped backbone with loops around it. A uniform lattice has no backbone.

Lynch ran the controlled experiment sixty-five years ago

Two Boston grids with the same topological form. Back Bay works; the South End does not. Lynch's account of the difference reads as a specification:

this is not a featureless regularity. The longitudinal streets were sharply differentiated from the cross streets in everyone's mind, much as they are in Manhattan… The relative width of the streets, the block lengths, the building frontages, the naming system, the relative length and number of the two kinds of streets, their functional importance, all tend to reinforce this differentiation. Thus a regular pattern is given form and character. Kevin Lynch, The Image of the City, 1960, pp. 60–61

Against the South End: "there is a lack of differentiated character in the major streets: Columbus Avenue is hard to distinguish from Tremont Street, or from Shawmut Avenue." And his verdict on the Los Angeles grid is the sentence for Borough — "this very regularity made it difficult for them to distinguish one path from another."

Corrects the brief My brief attributed the low-imageability case to a grid. It isn't one: Lynch chose Jersey City for "the complete confusion of an unco-ordinated street system." Los Angeles is the grid case. The distinction matters — Lynch's finding is that a grid is easy to navigate and weak as an image, which is a different failure from disorder.

What a real "uniform" grid actually contains

The Eixample — the most famous regular grid in Europe, and φ = 0.108 in its own city — described by researchers who measured it:

isotropic streets that are generally 20 metres wide and blocks that are 113 by 113 metres, with chamfered corners… two diagonals cross the area — Gaudí Avenue, 25 metres wide, and Enamorats street, 18 metres; six passageways between 3 and 8 metres wide subdivide some blocks…; and Montserrat Roig and Flora Tristan public gardens occupy two block cores. Gómez Escoda, Armengol & Berra-Sandín, Journal of Urban Design 28(4), 2023

That is the recipe in one paragraph. A uniform module, plus chamfers, plus two diagonals at two different widths, plus sub-block passages at a third width, plus two blocks replaced by gardens. Manhattan is the same shape: an 800×200 ft module, fifteen cross-streets widened to 100 ft, and Broadway — a pre-grid Lenape trail — cutting diagonally and generating Union, Madison, Herald, Times and Columbus at the crossings.

The lattice has already erased two gradients this project needed

Everything above is an argument from other people's cities. Borough has measured the same failure twice in its own logs, and in both cases the thing lost was not an appearance — it was a number somebody needed.

The first is in plans/0002 §D2. The volume-delay function's three hash-bearing parameters named a long run as their ratifier. The run happened. It could not fire:

A generated city's paved extent scales with the population it serves, so the same number sizes both the demand and the supply, v/c peaks at 0.44 at 4,000, 16,000 and 64,000 Citizens alike, and the denominator is never approached at any population this project can generate. plans/0002 §D2, 2026-08-15

The second is rulesets/platted.toml's own header. Its density band ring was denominated in the paved lattice's half-span, and the built extent is sized by the population — so the two scale together and no population ever crossed a boundary. The density ladder the file exists to demonstrate was invisible for most of that file's life, for exactly the reason the traffic curve was flat.

The generalisation, and it is not aesthetic

A generator that scales with what it serves erases the gradient it was built to expose. That is the same defect as ten thousand bowls of oatmeal — distinct in coordinates, identical in every respect anything can perceive — but it arrives here as two unratifiable hash-bearing numbers rather than as a dull picture. It is the strongest argument in the repository against machine-laid streets in Borough specifically, and none of it required an opinion about how a city should look.

The counterweight is real and belongs on the same page. docs/spike-results.md R0 swept the player-drawn network's cost at the shipped 32-Tile block: 33,018 Segments on a 4096² map, one Street on every Cell boundary, 16.20 km of road per km². The spike flags its own basis rather than defending it — that density is “a number to check against a real city, not a sweep”, and it sits against a corpus that justifies its target by citing Los Angeles sprawl at 3,700 people/km². Whether a player can be asked to draw 33,018 Segments is untested, and docs/01 asserts that a screen drag aggregating into many one-Segment commands is “a presentation question” with nothing behind it.

03

Hierarchy, and a spacing that derives rather than gets chosen

The US Federal Highway Administration publishes what a real urban network is made of. Two-thirds to three-quarters of urban street length carries under a quarter of the travel; four to five per cent of it carries a third.

ClassShare of urban mileageShare of travel
Interstate1–3%17–31%
Other principal arterial4–5%16–31%
Minor arterial7–14%14–25%
Collectors14–30%10–26%
Local63–75%6–25%

FHWA, Highway Functional Classification: Concepts, Criteria and Procedures, 2013 edition, Tables 3-5/3-6, from 2011 HPMS data. Ranges are independent and do not sum.

And on appearance, which is what this research is about — FHWA p. 19: "Even with a cursory view of a map of an urban area's roadway network, the functional classification of many roadways can be discerned due the differences in roadway size." That is exactly the property Borough's network lacks. Class is invisible because there is one class.

A spacing that derives from a stated ratio

FHWA states "the total Collector mileage is typically one-third of the Local roadway network." On a 128 m module, promoting every third lattice line to collector and every ninth to arterial gives:

Collector-to-local lands on 0.333 exactly, and 0.72 mile is inside FHWA's "should not exceed 1 mile in fully developed areas." These are derived, not chosen — which is the form adr/0052 asks for, and rare enough in this corpus to be worth saying out loud.

Two mechanisms worth stealing, both one line

Galin et al. (CGF 30(7), 2011) derive road class from what a road connects: τij = min(σi, σj), where σ is a settlement's size. Highways connect cities; major roads connect cities and towns; minor roads connect towns and villages. Width, lanes and markings all derive from τ. It is one line, integer-only, and Borough already has the σ side of it in building kinds, bands and District population.

Weber et al. (CGF 28(2), 2009) make the street's class an outcome: "A street will only be built when enough traffic demand is computed for the street. Then the street is marked as built and a temporary street width can be computed according to the current street.traffic value." Width and capacity are outputs of measured flow, not inputs. Borough has Trips, per-Segment flow and a BPR loop today — this is expressible now, and it makes hierarchy emerge rather than get configured.

04

Blocks and parcels: one change corroborated, one number exposed

Independently corroborated, seventeen years early

Weber et al. 2009, §5.3, equation 11: lot.nFloors = lot.floorspace / lot.envelope.area. That is exactly what plans/0058 did — a rung names a plot ratio, and storeys derive by dividing it by the share of block the pattern claims. The same identity, reached independently. It is a citable external ratifier for a number already in the code.

Sevtsuk, Kalvo & Ekmekci (Urban Morphology 20(2), 2016) measured seven historic grids. Plots per block frontage ranges from 4 (Chicago, Portland) to 32 (Manhattan). Borough's 128 m face at lots_per_segment = 5 gives about 25.6 m of frontage — Chicago is 24 m, Indianapolis 21 m. So Borough is a real city. It is one real city, permanently, everywhere.

Frontage also turns out to be the strongest lever the literature has on any measurable outcome. Cutting it from 10 m to 9 m raises pedestrian accessibility about 10%; from 50 m to 49 m, about 2%. The curve steepens below roughly 18 m. And critically, "having narrower plot frontages does not necessarily imply smaller blocks" — it is separable from the lattice.

One more: block depth is twice plot depth in six of the seven grids measured. Portland 30×2=60, Manhattan 30×2=60, Melbourne 100×2=200, Chicago 58×2≈115. Borough's BackToBack is not one of six equals — it is the empirically dominant real form, and the pattern weights may not reflect that.

Real plots are quantised, not equal — and the unit is per-block

Tait (Proc. Soc. Antiq. Scot. 138, 2008) re-measured 49 street blocks in Edinburgh, Canongate, St Andrews and Perth from the digitised OS first edition. Unit plot widths run 5.76 to 12.80 m, mean 8.09, median 8.11. Within a block, widths cluster at ¾, 1, 1¼, 1½, 1¾ and 2 × a block-specific unit — quantised, but not equal, and the modal peak is not always the largest. One St Andrews block peaks at 1¼; one Canongate sector contains no single-unit plots at all.

And the finding that matters most for a generator: there is no town-wide module. The unit itself varies 2.2× across the 49 blocks, and Tait rules out every standard measure — "there is no evidence for the use of the fall or the English perch… Even the use of the ell or yard would tend to produce a clustering of widths at regular intervals. Such clustering is not in evidence." His proposed mechanism is a rope of arbitrary length marked at quarter intervals, and his conclusion is a specification:

A formal system of measurement is not needed. Tait, 2008 — on how medieval burgage plots were actually set out

That is a counter-based integer generator described in six words. Draw a unit for the block; draw quarter-multiples of it for each plot along the face. No measurement system, no float, no world-wide constant. It is a better-evidenced mechanism than anything else this research turned up for parcels, and it fits Borough's determinism constraints exactly.

Two supporting facts. Depth is capped by access, and was known to be in 1903 — Hurd wrote that depth beyond 200–250 ft involves "a waste of land at the interior of the blocks owing to non-accessibility." Borough's 128 m block split back-to-back gives 64 m, about 210 ft, sitting right on that cap. And irregularity has a documented direction: front boundaries stay fixed while back boundaries drift, and boundary moves are asymmetric, producing "one larger and one much smaller plot" rather than two even ones.

Cities: Skylines II subdivides as value rises — Borough amalgamates

The CS2 asset documentation states the mechanism outright, and the cell size is confirmed by Colossal Order themselves in the UK Region Pack diary — "UK terraces are rarely as wide as 8 meters (1 game unit)." One cell is 8×8 m; the minimum lot is 1×2, so 8 m of frontage; the maximum is 6×6. The selection rule is published: "the zone algorithm always tries to spawn the largest possible lot. It first selects the deepest option, then the widest."

And the direction of change: "the zoning algorithm will adapt by subdividing larger plot areas into smaller lots" as land value rises. SimCity 4 goes further and re-cuts both ways — its official guide describes the simulator "parcelizing" within a zone and then, as conditions change, redrawing "the dimensions of the lot to be larger (aggregation) or smaller (subdivision)."

Borough only coarsens. ParcelsPerFace hands one Address the ground several would have divided as the rung climbs, and nothing ever re-divides. The historical record says subdivision is the dominant process and amalgamation a nineteenth-century latecomer — so Borough's density ladder runs the burgage cycle backwards, and it runs it in one direction where both shipped comparators run in two. That is worth deciding deliberately rather than inheriting.

Method abandoned Modern quantitative morphology has dropped frontage-to-depth ratio as a metric altogether, because real plots are not rectangles (Bobkova, Berghauser Pont & Marcus, 2021). Borough's parcels are rectangles, so the ratio remains meaningful here — but it is worth knowing the field stopped using it.

What survives integer-only determinism

Borough bans floating point in simulation, bans random streams, bans hash-map enumeration. That filter kills less than expected and kills the field's headline technique outright.

The straight skeleton is dead for a reason that has nothing to do with floats

It is the primary method in the state-of-the-art parcel paper. Huber's thesis rules it out on stability: the skeleton "does not continuously depend on the input graph", and "Eppstein and Erickson remarked that perturbation techniques cannot be applied… such situations are very likely to occur, in particular if P contains collinear edges." City blocks are axis-aligned and made of collinear edges — the degenerate case is the normal case. CGAL with exact predicates runs quadratic and hit 3,197 MB at n=8192.

Two things make this painless. The paper that uses it prefers the alternative anyway: "while we could use the straight skeleton to generate the initial split line, using the block's OBB turns out in practice to yield a more robust mechanism." And on an axis-aligned square block the skeleton degenerates to the two diagonals — which BlockPattern.cs already refused, on the right grounds: "A geometry that tiles is not thereby a geometry that is built."

WFC's failure mode is measured, and the fix is free

Merrell's comparison, Table 1: success rates of 0.2%, 2.7%, 10.4%, 1.8%, 45.9% — and 0% on eight of eighteen rows after 1,000 to 10,000 trials. "Model synthesis can generate in a few seconds what WFC fails to generate in over 20 minutes." A world-creation step that may never terminate is incompatible with a deterministic budget. The repair is to sweep in scanline order rather than picking lowest entropy — which removes the failure cliff and the algorithm's only floating-point term at the same time.

Three premises in the brief that the record does not support

Corrects the brief Factorio did not keep floats where it mattered. Its official Lua API says MapPosition is "a fixed-size 32 bit integer, with 8 bits reserved for decimal precision" — Q24.8. It chose fixed point for the hash-critical quantity and paid separately to make floats deterministic elsewhere, including writing its own trigonometry because "basic trigonometric functions are not guaranteed to give same results across different platforms."

Unverified "Age of Empires used fixed point" is not supported by Bettner & Terrano's article, which makes no such statement. And Dwarf Fortress claims far less than folklore: no primary statement from its author that worldgen is reproducible across hardware or versions. On the published record it is seed-parameterised generation, not a determinism guarantee.

06

What nobody in the industry does

A survey of twenty shipped city-builders found that press coverage conflates three completely different things. Separating them is the useful result.

What the machine doesGamesWhose network is it?
Snaps roads to buildings the player placed Anno 117 (one-click surround), Tropico 6 (aprons at any rotation) The player's
Routes a road the player asked for The Settlers II, Anno 117, Cities: Skylines II Grid mode The player's
Lays out a network the player never drew SimCity 4, Frostpunk 2, Foundation, Ostriv — plus OpenTTD and Simutrans, where towns build their own The machine's

The strongest and least-known case is SimCity 4, from its own manual: "Secondary streets are automatically constructed in any new zone." The player's zone rectangle is the input and a street grid is the output — so the player's later road-drawing is an upgrade pass over machine-laid geometry, not the original authorship.

Frostpunk 2 generates the layout inside a player-drawn district, and shipped something revealing: an opt-in "legacy urban layout". Changing the road generator changed players' cities enough that the studio had to ship a compatibility switch. Generated layout is noticed.

Corrects what I published an hour ago I wrote that Ostriv and Foundation "delete the network entirely." Both halves are wrong. Ostriv's traffic-worn paths are simulation state with a consequence — "Hay dryers are now sensitive to grass trampling around them" — and it later added player-built paved roads on top. Foundation shipped a road paint tool at 1.0, gated behind a housing tier: a game whose identity was "no roads" gave the player a road brush. The drift is toward more player authorship, not less.

A refusal, which under adr/0070 is the one classification that is evidence

Manor Lords built the desire-path system and threw it away. Its developer, in full:

To boost the organic look of towns I added an eroding paths system. The paths were created wherever peasants walked. However during a long gameplay it did not quite look as cool as I expected… Turns out if you simply erode the grass wherever peasants walk, after a long time you get weird geometric results. Due to the size of the map the resolution of the path texture had to be high and was never enough. I ended up scrapping this system and instead take more care to place more small paths between buildings in a more controlled fashion. Slavic Magic, 2022-07-09

Two named failure modes: long-run accumulation produces geometric rather than organic artefacts, and the texture resolution required does not fit a large map. And a vestige survives — patch notes still optimise "marketplace dirt erosion" — so erosion was kept where traffic is dense and spatially bounded, and dropped where it is sparse and map-wide. Anyone reaching for emergent paths in Borough should read this first.

Ostriv refused the other half too, and gave a reason: "it could even be generated automatically based on model geometry. But that would take away from Ostriv its unique handcrafted feel." An explicit, reasoned refusal to derive building footprints — the opposite decision to Borough's, argued rather than defaulted.

Caesar III splits authorship the opposite way from everyone, which is worth holding as a shape: the player draws road geometry and the machine owns road quality — "citizens pave roads when surrounding property becomes sufficiently desirable" — while the player designates land and the machine owns buildings. Its access rule is a hard number from the manual: "Houses need to be within two squares of a road."

The flagship failure, and it is exactly this shape

Introversion's Subversion had the best procedural city generator indie games had produced: rules governing road growth where "by making small changes to those rules, you can vastly change the result" — Manhattan grids against radial patterns around population centres — generating "a complete city from the satellite view down to individual buildings" over a 10 km square, with "procedural generators for every single step of the process." Five years of work. Chris Delay's own verdict, on the day it was cancelled in 2011:

But there was a massive gaping hole where you would normally see a "core game". Chris Delay, Introversion Software, 2011-10-17

The generator was never the missing part.

And the industry's direction of travel runs the other way from this research. Foundation — sold as "grid-less… with a heavy focus on organic development", whose identity was you don't build roads — shipped a road brush at 1.0 giving the player "complete control of where you wish paved roads to be created." Frostpunk 2 added manual Plaza placement. Nobody in the twenty-game survey moved toward more generation.

So adr/0090 puts Borough in the majority, not in a unique position. The unusual thing about Borough is not that the player draws the roads — it is that the player cannot draw an Arterial.

Borough's own corpus already settles the design question

01 §683: "An overlay must never be sharper than the player's ability to act on it… Showing a problem with no corresponding verb is an invitation to frustration." A generated street network that the game explains perfectly, set against a verb that lays one Segment per click, is an overlay sharper than the verb — the failure the corpus already names. And under adr/0070, the answer to given the road verb is thin, should the machine compensate? is build the verb.

One thing the survey does add, which adr/0090 did not price: a full map is about 525,000 Segments. That ADR argued about permission — may the player build anywhere — and never argued about labour. SimCity 4's answer (the player lays through-routes and zones an area; the machine laces the area with lattice edges) is the shape that fits. Under the amnesty that is a decision to record as owed, not to take.

⚠ And SimCity 4's own documentation records the mechanism's failure mode, which is the part worth carrying with it: "When zoning, Streets are drawn automatically to give each lot transportation access. This auto-drawing should make transportation a non-issue, but it occasionally leaves isolated lots without access. In this case, you must manually draw the Street out to the disconnected lot." The machine laces, and sometimes the player has to go in and repair it by hand.

One pillar of Borough's, stated by the architect of GlassBox

Andrew Willmott's GDC 2012 deck names the failure GlassBox was built to fix — "Our past games have been primarily statistical… makes it hard for player to understand what's going on with their sim (SimCity 4 traffic system)" — and states the repair as a slogan: "Rather than visualise game statistics, show actual game state. Show cars instead of traffic density… Ensure cause and effect is obvious: What You See Is What You Sim."

That is LEGIBLE CAUSE in someone else's words, and Librande supplies the thing it was reacting against: in the SimCity games before 2013, "the streets are just showing the state of the adjacent buildings. The cars are simply graphical feedback and aren't actually part of the simulation." The fake Borough refuses by construction was the genre standard for two decades.

The finding that cuts against this session's own direction

Manor Lords tried procedural buildings and abandoned them. Its developer, in 2022: "I went through a lot of iterations of late game towns. I tried procedural generation but ended up using a more standard approach with randomized pre-made buildings."

Its stated cosmetic fix for monotony is also plot-level: on wide plots the game "will sometimes leave a facade gap and pick smaller house varieties." That is one team's answer after trying it the other way, and it points the variety budget somewhere this session has not been spending it.

And I over-compressed it "Abandoned procedural buildings" is too flat, and a later agent caught me. What Manor Lords rejected is procedural mesh generation. What it ships is procedural fitting and assembly — and the proof is a bug report: "Fixed oversized Tier 4 Burgage Plots caused by using meters instead of centimetres in the scaling algorithm." A unit-conversion bug in a scaling algorithm only exists if house dimensions are computed against plot dimensions at runtime. Gates, fences, side sheds, ground blends, pathfinding nodes and the garden's patch tiles are all spawned against the plot polygon too. The library is authored; the placement is algorithmic. Quoting either half alone misrepresents the game.

The price of a free-form parcel, in the developer's own notes

Manor Lords' flexible plots forced it to abandon radius-based adjacency effects entirely. Verbatim: "We prototyped what building games usually do — effects applied in a radius, or in specific zones, but found out that the Manor Lords' flexible plot system doesn't play very well with placing stuff in areas, especially when they are circular. Fitting rectangular plots into circles is not pleasant." They replaced radius effects with region-scale environment types. Borough runs several radius mechanisms — the 400 m Parking Shed, the pollution kernel, land value — so this is a direct warning about a combination it has not tried.

And the robustness bill is itemised across three years of patch notes: triangular-plot crashes, non-deterministic gate spawning on narrow plots, props levitating and spawning underground, fences vanishing on upgrade, houses pushing too deep into the plot, and extension geometry misaligning when the plot's centre line and the extension's diverge. Planting cost was computed from the extension's perimeter rather than its area for roughly eighteen months of shipped game. Free-form parcels are not free.

One place Borough is genuinely ahead. Congestion does not feed back into routing anywhere in the genre — Cities: Skylines' own wiki says vehicles take the fastest legal route and "it takes no account of other traffic." Borough's BPR loop priced on entry already does what the genre leaders do not, which means a road hierarchy would carry more consequence here than it does in Skylines.

07

What to do, ranked

Ordered by evidence-per-unit-of-work. None of the top three needs a geometry primitive Borough does not have — and it has exactly one, SqrtFloor.

1

Make an empty world reachable in the shell

Main.cs:897 issues Populate at Tick 0 with no guard. Until that is a flag, nobody — including you — can look at a city a player built, and the road verb has never been observed working. Every screenshot, every balance run and every judgement about how the city reads has been taken on a synthetic checkerboard.

This is not a design question. It is a start-mode flag, and it gates the honesty of every other item on this page — including move 2, which measures a graph the generator laid rather than one a player did.

It also has a named reading waiting for it, and the reading is not aesthetic. A hand-drawn network can be wrong, which the generated one structurally cannot be. So the question to put to the first empty world is: does v/c ever exceed 0.44? If it does, two hash-bearing traffic parameters that have been stuck since 2026-08-15 acquire the world their ratifier names. If instead the player builds one blob beside one gate on a 65.5 km map, that is evidence too — and it is the first evidence anyone has had either way.

Cost: a flag. Highest value on this page for the least work.

2

Run OSMnx against Borough's own graph

DONE 2026-09-04, and it refuted three of the figures it was built to confirm — see section 01.

Built as Borough.Headless --morphology rather than as OSMnx, because osmnx is not installed here and pulling in geopandas and GDAL to measure a graph the project already holds in memory is a dependency nobody needed. It computes bearing distribution over 36 bins, orientation entropy both unweighted and length-weighted, φ, per-Segment circuity, the node degree histogram, four-way share over intersections and over nodes, and intersection density per km² and per square mile.

The entropy is now verified against its source and the index still is not — checked against osmnx/bearing.py, the paper author's own implementation. Four things match: 36 bins is that function's default; bearings are bidirectional, two reciprocal per undirected edge; the distribution is unweighted by default; the entropy is in nats.

One thing didn't, and it was a real defect. The reference bins are centred on the compass points rather than starting at them — it histograms into 72 half-bins, rolls by one and sums adjacent pairs, "so eg 0.01 deg and 359.99 deg will be binned together". This build split them, so one direction landed at both ends of the histogram: entropy invented out of a boundary. Fixed — the bin table now reads 355°–5° for north and wraps. It changed none of the headline figures, because a lattice's bearings are exactly 0/90/180/270 and land cleanly under either partition — which is precisely the problem with it. A boundary error is invisible on data that doesn't sit near the boundary, and a curving Arterial does.

What remains unverified is φ = 1 − ((H − Hg)/(Hmax − Hg))² with Hg = ln 4 and Hmax = ln 36. OSMnx doesn't implement φ, and the paper sits behind Springer's authorisation wall — four routes to a readable copy returned no text. The two anchors and the square are recalled, not sourced, and all three are named at their definitions so a reader with the paper can check three lines.

Cost: one Headless mode and four assertion-tier tests. None of them pins a figure.

3

Give the Arterial a producer

The class exists, its speed and capacity are wired, and the BPR loop would price it today. Nothing can make one. Whether that producer is the player (adr/0077 reopened) or a derivation in Galin's shape — a Segment's class from what it joins — is a design question, but the current state is a mechanism with no door.

Cost: an argument, then a small change. The data model is already there.

4

Jitter the lattice spacing — for blocks, not for φ

A per-row and per-column spacing offset under a new purpose_tag, keyed on the row index. Blocks stay axis-aligned, so LotSubdivider, Frontage, BlockPattern and parity house-numbering are all untouched. Because six patterns already select on block size, varying size cascades into pattern variety for free.

It will not move φ at all. Every street stays axis-aligned, so all bearings stay in four bins. Worth doing; not a fix for section 01.

Cost: zero new geometry primitives. A spacing table on the street grid.

5

Spend the appearance budget on the ground, not on more meshes

Ground() draws one slab textured at 512×512 — one texel per Cell, one texel per 128 m, bilinearly filtered, so inside a block the ground is a single interpolated colour. Scatter() places trees and rocks by two independent 12-bit fractions with no minimum distance and no per-instance colour at all. The MultiMesh already carries a colour channel that scatter spends on nothing.

Rabii & Cook (FDG 2023) argue against the obvious alternative: adding more meshes enlarges the possibility space "without adding any new encoded knowledge", which is the branch that produces oatmeal.

Cost: no new meshes. Higher-resolution skin, per-instance colour, relaxed spacing.

6

Replace lots_per_segment with a per-block unit and quarter multiples

This is the best-evidenced mechanism in the whole of this research, and it is the one Borough's constraints suit best. Tait's 49 re-measured Scottish blocks say plots are quantised multiples of a unit that belongs to the block, not to the town — ¾, 1, 1¼, 1½, 1¾, 2 — with the unit itself varying 2.2× block to block and no standard measure underneath it.

So: draw a unit per block under its own purpose_tag, then draw quarter multiples along each face until the frontage is consumed. "A formal system of measurement is not needed." Fixed equal lots per segment is refuted by the evidence; fixed quantised lots is close to right, which is why this is a small change rather than a rewrite.

Siksna's dynamic rules layer on top and are directly implementable — subdivision occurs along the wider frontages first, and 4- or 8-lot square blocks amalgamate cleanly where more than 8 do not.

Cost: one new purpose tag and an integer draw. No geometry, no float, no world-wide constant.

And two things not to do

Do not change block_tiles. 128 m is inside the real range on every size measure. The problem is variation, not grain.

Do not build the straight skeleton. Its instability on collinear edges is structural, the paper that uses it prefers OBB anyway, and on a square block it degenerates to a geometry BlockPattern.cs already rejected by argument.

08

What was not established

Recorded because a labelled gap is worth more than a confident guess, and because the previous batch's worst error was a figure taken from a search summary rather than a source.

Unverified Allan Jacobs' Great Streets figures. The book was located and its structure confirmed; the text was inaccessible. Every intersections-per-square-mile figure associated with it — Venice, Rome, Portland, Irvine, Brasília — is unobtained and none has been reproduced here. Boeing 2019 is the substitute and is arguably better for this purpose.

Unverified Blue noise "reads better" for scatter. Bridson's justification is rendering-sampling theory, not perception. Practitioners do it; no primary source claims it looks better.

Looked for three times, found none of them Nobody has published the argument that players draw roads for agency or self-expression. Three agents went after it independently — through Colossal Order's dev diaries, Korppoo's TED talk and GDC session, Paradox studio statements, and the complete Steam announcement corpora for both Cities: Skylines games — and neither found it stated. The nearest is metaphor and ordering: roads get dev diary #1, and Colossal Order calls them "the backbone of a city" and zones "the meat fleshing out the city." The agency reading is inference, not a quote, and this page does not rest on it.

And this negative was already in the repository. docs/references.md §9: “No developer rationale for the SC4 → SC2013 shift could be found. Quigley's GDC 2013 talk, Librande's BLDGBLOG interview, and Willmott's Inside GlassBox were all checked; none discusses it. Treat this as an open gap rather than an absence of reasons.” — and on CS1/CS2 zoning, “no developer commentary on that choice was found for either title.” Fifteen agents re-derived a result this project had written down and dated. The finding is stronger for having been reached twice by different routes; the search was still redundant, and the corpus said so before it started.

But two studios did publish an argument — both for the other side Foundation's Kickstarter states the case for machine layout outright: "Too often city-builders generate squarish cities that seem to be designed by a soviet urbanist of the 20th century… Buildings back then were constructed by individuals, not architects, they were placed messily and close to each other." And Frostpunk 2's game directors, asked in a Reddit AMA whether the shift to districts cost the player agency, answered by redefining the word:

If by that you mean micro control over building placement then no, this has not changed. However, if you mean how much your city-planning and city-building choices impact gameplay, then I'd say that there's a very large amount of agency in the game. Jakub Stokalski, Game Director, Frostpunk 2, 2024-09-10

He concedes the loss plainly in the same thread — "if what you loved about FP1 was single building placement, radial grid — well yes, some things are gone" — and argues the trade was worth it because consequence replaced placement. That is the strongest published statement of the position opposite to move 3 on this page, and it deserves to be read before anyone settles Borough's line.

That search did establish a firm negative, though. Grepping both games' full announcement corpora for procedural, auto-generat and road generation returns zero hits. No auto-road or district-generation feature has ever shipped in either Cities: Skylines.

Unverified No developer statement anywhere on what detail stops reading at a far camera. The one adjacent data point: Colossal Order's themes vary street markings and roadside props, not asphalt, kerb profile or wear.

Unverified Vanegas et al. 2012 itself — the state-of-the-art parcel paper — is behind Wiley. Kelly's PhD thesis carries his half verbatim, so the skeleton algorithm is read and the OBB half is known only by reference.

Premise wrong Ostriv has no terrain devblog. An agent walked the entire archive back to October 2016. My brief asserted one existed.

Folklore, and wrong Amsterdam did not tax houses by facade width. This is a famous story and the record contradicts it. Abrahamse's 2010 dissertation on the canal belt, built on the city archive's own resolutions, describes the verponding as a real-estate tax assessed by house count — and the word gevelbreedte appears zero times in 345 pages. The documented mechanism runs the other way: the city decreed plot widths when it sold the land — 30 voet in 1613, 26 and 22 in 1663 — and narrowed them in 1663 explicitly to discourage subdivision. Narrow Amsterdam houses are a planning decision, not a tax dodge.

Do not use Conzen's "28 feet at Alnwick" — the most-quoted burgage figure in circulation. Conzen 1960 itself is lending-restricted and could not be read; every figure attributed to it here is attested through a later scholar citing a page. The 28-feet number traces to a single amateur blog post that contains an acknowledged arithmetic error. Tait 2008 is the source to use instead, and it is open access.

No published number exists Manor Lords has never published a burgage plot's minimum frontage, minimum area, or the thresholds a backyard extension needs. The game communicates fit through icons in the placement preview. Every figure in circulation is either datamined and unattributed, or measured by players using a corpse pit as a ruler. One number does appear in the patch notes — "lowering the standard plot width from 1000 to 750" — but it sits in the AI-opponent section, is not a player-facing limit, and states no unit. Reading it as 7.5 m is an inference, not a source.

Nobody has ever built an evaluation system for procedural block subdivision — Kelly says so explicitly. So there is no established way to say whether Borough's six patterns are good, and the metrics on this page are all network metrics borrowed from elsewhere.
