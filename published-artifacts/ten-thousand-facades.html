Ten Thousand Facades

Borough can’t fake its buildings much longer. This is what procedural generation can do about that, what it can’t, and which of the corpus’s reference citations survive being checked.

The verdict

In the corpus

Procedural is right for Borough, and it isn’t really a choice

docs/07-the-drawing.md §3 already decided this. It picks pipeline C: hand-author pieces at a fixed module, assemble them with code. Nothing found here overturns that decision.

What did not survive is the evidence the document cites for it. Seven reference games are named in §4, each “cited for a specific property,” under an explicit caveat that none had been re-checked against a running copy. That caveat turns out to have been carrying the whole table.

And the corpus argues the case on the wrong ground. §3 prices the three pipelines by labour and scale — you can’t author enough buildings, and a million can’t each carry a mesh. True, and pillar 1.4 makes it binding, but that argument would hold for a city builder with no simulation under it. The stronger version is in Fit for Borough.

Generation, generally

New here

The useful frame isn’t “how do I generate a building”

It’s generative space — the set of everything a generator can produce — and the two ways that space fails.

Failure one: the space is too small

Everything looks the same. Obvious and self-correcting. You see it in a screenshot and go widen a range.

Failure two: the space is enormous and nobody can tell

Kate Compton named this the 10,000 bowls of oatmeal problem. You can generate ten thousand bowls in which every oat sits at a mathematically unique position, and a player sees porridge. Mathematical uniqueness is free; perceptual uniqueness is the only one that counts, and it is hard.

A shader that varies bay width, storey count, roof pitch and paint across a hundred thousand boxes produces enormous variety and may still read as one texture at the camera height docs/07 §2 commits to.

The consequence for Borough’s grammar, which is specific

Every quantity the shader varies today is continuous, and every one of them varies a single template. That is the exact recipe for oatmeal: jitter parameters on one shape and you get objects that are each unique and collectively identical.

The fix is not a wider range. It is categorical branches — different massing and roofline rules per kind, so a school and a terrace differ in shape rather than in dimensions. That is a change to the grammar’s structure, not to its constants, and it is the cheapest unspent lever on kind identity. Roofline before colour, because silhouette reads first at distance.

The other half of the standard fix is authored landmarks against a procedural field — a few hand-made things that break the field and give the eye somewhere to land. That is docs/07 §3.1’s tier 2, and the oatmeal argument makes it more load-bearing than “kinds announce themselves” sounds: it is the specific defence against the failure mode tier 1 has by construction.

The trade underneath all of it

Procedural generation is a compression technique. Pay authoring cost once for a rule, get many outputs. What you give up is the ability to art-direct any individual output: if one building looks wrong you cannot fix that building, you fix the rule and move all of them. That trade is the entire decision, regardless of engine, style or scale.

Manor Lords does not generate its buildings

It draws plots procedurally and stamps a small set of hand-made house meshes onto them, chosen by upgrade tier. Four converging lines:

  • Frontage is a module. A single-width plot is one family slot at about 9 m. A double-width plot holds two houses, not one wide one. Width adds instances; a grammar would stretch the facade.
  • The mesh is keyed to tier. Levels 1, 2 and 3 are distinct models, with artisan workshop pieces added separately.
  • The complaint is the signature. “Stamps out rows of identical looking plots with identical houses in the exact same orientation.”
  • The fix shipped as art. Patch 0.8.017 updated residential visuals for Levels 1 and 2. Somebody remade meshes.

What is procedural there is the ground: plots drawn from four points, edges conforming to the roads, leftover depth filling with gardens and sheds. So the closest shipped analogue is strong evidence for pillar 1.2 and none at all for tier 1.

Townscaper is a kit of parts, not a massing proof

Stålberg’s own description: he builds square tiles by hand, skews them to fit, and feeds them to wave function collapse, which “figures out how to create a coherent environment without any gaps.” He is explicit that tile count scales with variety — “the more things it has to support, the more puzzle pieces you need to build.” Roughly 500 pieces, authored in Maya, edge-scanned on import to derive adjacency rules.

So “effectively no unique assets” is false, and the citation points at the wrong tier. Townscaper is evidence for the kit of parts — pipeline C, which Borough already picked. What is genuinely procedural is the grid: a hex chunk filled with triangles, randomly paired into quads, then relaxed. A placement technique with nothing to do with mesh generation.

SimCity 4 is not sprites

It was the first main-series SimCity to use a 3D engine: real-time polygonal buildings with textures adding doors and windows, under a fixed trimetric orthographic camera, billboards only for the cheapest clutter. The sprite description belongs to SimCity 3000.

Which improves the citation rather than destroying it. The doc credits SC4 for a technique Borough cannot use; the mechanism SC4 actually used — real-time textured geometry, fixed high camera, billboards for clutter — is close to what Borough does today.

The window-lighting claim is the one that matters

docs/07 §2 says a lit city at night is wanted and “the windows are lit from occupancy,” and §4 cites CS2 as the precedent. Borough has since built exactly that.

There is no precedent. CS2’s window glow is a screen-space emissive post-process driven by a greyscale illumination texture, with lit-ness “randomized for values within 128 and 255” — a time-of-day cosmetic, inherited in shape from CS1. A later patch note describes assets “configured with windows that should always remain lit or unlit,” which is an author-time flag, not a simulation hook. And Anno’s developers considered and rejected the idea outright: “we played around with ideas how the shift from day to the midst of the night could affect gameplay, and while it would open many possibilities, we realized that doing so would simply add a lot of unnecessary micromanagement.” Its night cycle added “new glowing textures for illuminated windows” and over a thousand light sources, all cosmetic.

Both citations lean on the same idea and neither game does it. So Borough’s occupancy-lit windows are not a copy of anything — they are original, and the corpus was giving away credit it had earned.

What Anno actually proves, and it isn’t what the citation says

Tier distinction is real and deliberate: “Every house, public and even production building for each tier should convey a certain character,” and “not only the 3D models, the right palette of colour and material also defines look and feeling of a tier.” But the model counts are published — tiers 1–3 have four models each, tiers 4 and 5 have six, so roughly 24 hand-authored residential meshes for the Old World.

That is a curated library, and it is the pipeline Borough cannot afford. The transferable half is the colour and material coding by kind, which is cheap, shader-friendly and scales.

What §4 should become

The rewrite is structural rather than row-by-row. Each surviving citation needs splitting into the outcome (which is usually real and worth wanting) and the mechanism (which is usually a hand-authored library Borough cannot copy). Manor Lords moves to pillar 1.2 and the ground. Townscaper moves to tier 2 and the kit. SimCity 4 keeps its property and loses its explanation. The CS2 lighting line is deleted and the credit reassigned to Borough.

The shader ceiling

New here

The ceiling is silhouette, and that explains everyone else’s pipeline

A fragment shader cannot move the rasterised outline of a cube. Roofs, chimneys, spires, parapets, setbacks, balconies — geometry or nothing.

Which answers a question the grammar research raised. Nobody ships a live shape-grammar interpreter: Watch Dogs wrote CGA-like split rules and baked textures and LODs offline; CityEngine’s Unreal plugin is in practice an editor-time bake tool; Spider-Man used procedural authoring tools and shipped baked levels; Cyberpunk 2077 went fully hand-made, on the record — “nothing is procedural in our world”; Citybound used a grammar and never shipped.

They all bake because they treat runtime procedural as regenerating geometry, and they want geometry because they want silhouette. Borough’s shader evaluates the grammar as a shading function over a fixed box, which sidesteps that cost model entirely — and hits precisely the wall the baking bought them past.

So the approach is genuinely novel in the region where silhouette doesn’t matter, and structurally incapable in the region where it does. No free lunch, and no missed trick either.

Interior mapping is the technique worth taking

Van Dongen, 2008. Model the interior as evenly-spaced axis-aligned planes, which turns the problem into closed-form ray–plane intersection rather than iterative raymarching: for each axis compute where the ray next crosses a boundary, take the smallest positive t, use the hit’s local coordinate as a UV. It is cheaper than parallax occlusion mapping precisely because it is exact rather than stepped.

It lands on a pixel Borough already owns. The shader has decided a pane is lit; instead of filling a warm rectangle, cast the ray and shade the hit. A handful of divides and compares per lit fragment, behind a branch that already exists, touching nothing in daylight or on an unlit wall.

Two honesties. Every documented implementation samples a texture at the hit point — a texture-free variant (tint by axis, plus a hashed SDF furniture blob) is a synthesis, not something anyone has published. And the known failure is corner rooms showing the same interior through both windows, plus flat furniture close up. Neither survives contact with the 500–1100 m fade.

The cheapest silhouette variety is more roof meshes

Borough already instances a prism gable separately. That is the well-trodden route, not a stopgap: SimCity and Watch Dogs both used a small library of feature meshes placed onto procedurally-subdivided blockouts, explicitly to solve the roofs and non-rectangular faces a shader could not reach. No shipped example of vertex-shader box displacement for roofline variety turned up.

So the marginal move is more roof archetypes — hip, gable, flat, mansard as separate small meshes — rather than a cleverer shader. Which is the same lever the legibility research pointed at for kind identity, arrived at from the opposite direction.

Control grammars: the idea transfers, the code does not exist

Wonka’s 2003 control grammar has essentially one follow-up — inverse design of urban procedural models, 2012 — and it is one lab’s prototype with no public library. Twenty years, and nobody built the reusable version.

The minimal implementation is cheap and fits what Borough has: derive a style-parameter vector deterministically from each District’s own id, and let the shader read it beside the transform. That is a control grammar. It costs a design decision, not a pipeline.

Findings that bite

New here

Four engine constraints the plan doesn’t know about

1. Shadow casting is per-node, not per-instance

cast_shadow lives on GeometryInstance3D, and a MultiMeshInstance3D is one node covering its whole instance set. There is no way to say “this building casts, that one doesn’t.”

Given that CS2 died in the shadow pass and Borough now has a moving sun, the consequence is structural: the shadow-caster radius must be a separate, smaller set of Chunks than the render radius, tuned against directional_shadow_max_distance. That wants deciding before the chunked renderer is built. And distance fade cannot do the job — issue #97537 has Distance Fade stopping shadow casting outright; the docs recommend DISABLED fade mode anyway, since the alternatives force alpha-blend transparency.

2. No per-instance LOD, and chunking is the engine’s own answer

Godot’s MultiMesh docs state it directly: there is no screen or frustum culling possible for individual instances, and “millions of objects will be always or never drawn, depending on the visibility of the whole MultiMesh.” The documented fix is “several MultiMeshes for different areas of the world.”

That is 05 §5 validated by the engine’s own documentation rather than by inference. Proposal #10669, asking for per-instance culling and LOD, is open with no maintainer engagement. Its benchmark figures name no machine and no thread count, so they are unquotable under adr/0106; they indicate a direction and nothing more.

3. Godot 4 has no working GPU-driven culling path

Writing a MultiMesh transform buffer from a compute shader does not render unless multimesh_set_buffer is also called with a CPU-side array — three open issues confirm the GPU→GPU path lacks synchronisation and forces a round trip. So every culling and LOD decision is CPU-side, per Chunk, at whatever cadence the frame can afford. There is no Nanite-style escape.

That makes the bookkeeping cost above real rather than theoretical. RenderingServer.multimesh_set_buffer() is the fast bulk path, and visible_instance_count shrinks an active set without reallocating.

4. INSTANCE_CUSTOM is four floats, and Borough has spent all four

One Color per instance, confirmed current, with proposal #8666 open and unresolved. Godot’s own documentation names the escape hatch: a floating-point Image indexed per instance, not a second custom-data channel. Instance uniforms will not help — they cap around 16 scalars and cannot carry a sampler.

So the route to a fifth channel exists and is available now rather than pending a proposal. Worth knowing before the next thing that needs one, which on current evidence is the control-grammar style vector.

One honest non-answer. Whether procedural-in-shader beats sampled textures at high instance counts has no sourced conclusion either way. That is a measurement Borough would have to take.

Fit for Borough

New here In the corpus

The real argument isn’t cost. It’s that an authored building is mute.

Pillar 1.3 says the picture says what the simulation knows. The corpus never uses that pillar as the pipeline argument, and it is the one that actually decides.

adr/0150 derives appearance from six live simulation quantities: declared occupancy, the tenant’s trade, Failure Pressure, frontage, the land-value Cell under the footprint, and a per-Building draw. A hand-authored mesh cannot express any of them.

What the city knows An authored mesh A grammar
Storeys Fixed at author time — cannot report the ladder plans/0056 just made variable Recovered exactly from the box height
Occupancy Fixed window count — cannot show a quarter half-let Share of openings shuttered
Condition Needs a swap to a “derelict” variantAsset pools, through the art door Read off the paint, recovers instantly
Frontage Needs the world quantised to fit the mesh Whatever width the Lot has

The third row is the trap worth naming. Making condition a variant swap is adr/0025’s mutually-exclusive asset pools arriving through the art door — what adr/0150 warned about, reached by a route it wasn’t watching.

Procedural isn’t the affordable option for Borough. It’s the only one that can satisfy pillar 1.3 at all. The authored library isn’t rejected because it’s expensive. It’s rejected because it can’t say anything.

The audit strengthens this rather than weakening it. Every game checked that looks good does it with a curated library — Anno’s 24 meshes, Townscaper’s 500 tiles, Manor Lords’ tier prefabs, CS’s pooled growables — and not one of them reports anything about its simulation through the building’s appearance. The two that were cited as reporting occupancy turn out not to.

Three caveats, all real

  • It holds for massing and condition. It holds not at all for identity — no grammar says school. That is tier 2, it must be authored, and the oatmeal argument says it is more load-bearing than docs/07 §2 makes it sound.
  • The ceiling on charm in §3’s row A is real, and it is now named precisely: it is the silhouette. What changed since 2006 is not the technique but that GPUs made per-fragment grammars viable at all.
  • The closest analogues all chose prefabs and they look better. That is evidence and it does not disappear because the reason is scale. Manor Lords is a few hundred buildings; Borough targets a million and prefabs do not survive that. But the trade reversing at scale is Borough’s bet, and nobody has shipped a proof.
The warning about timing is the one to hold onto. Manor Lords’ “identical houses in the exact same orientation” complaint arrives at a few hundred buildings. Sameness reads far earlier than instance counts suggest.
