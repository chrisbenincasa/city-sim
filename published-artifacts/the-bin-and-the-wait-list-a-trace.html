Session N1 · task 1 · the Bin half

A Rule asleep beside the thing it needs

One mechanism, traced end to end with the shipped Ruleset's own numbers — because it has never been written down in one place, and four of the sentences that describe it are stale.

You said the invariants feel circular and spread across too many documents. They are. This mechanism is currently assembled from CONTEXT.md, 02 §3–§4, four ADRs and a handful of doc-comments, and no single place traces it. That is why it is hard to reason about, and it is not a failure of attention on your part.

So: the cast, the loop, the working case, the broken case, and then the decision — with every number taken from rulesets/minimal.toml and every behaviour read off the code rather than the prose.

01The cast — five things, and only five

Everything in this argument is one of these. If a document introduces a sixth, it is describing something that does not exist yet.

ThingWhat it holdsThe one fact that matters here
Bin {owner, resource, capacity, level} plus four wait-list links An integer store, [0, capacity]. One writer, and the write drains the wait list
Rule Inputs and outputs, a rate, an apply count Declared in the Ruleset. Applies in its entirety or not at all
Rule Instance One Rule on one Building — where it has got to Armed or waiting, never both. The two states share one link field, so "both" is unrepresentable
Level wait list Consumers that found too little stock Blocked reason Level. Drained by a deposit
Headroom wait list Producers that found no room Blocked reason Headroom. Drained by a withdrawal

Two Bins, two lists each. A Rule that fails names one Bin and one reason, and sleeps on that list holding the quantity it was short of.

02The crux: the Goods arrive before anyone decides who wakes

This is the single fact that reframes the whole question, and it is four lines of code.

// World.cs:994 — the only way a Bin's level ever moves upward
public void Deposit(Handle<Bin> bin, int amount, Ticks tick)
{
    Bins.Move(slot, amount);                      // 1003 — the level rises FIRST
    Drain(slot, Blocking.Level, amount, tick);    // 1004 — then we choose who wakes
}

So "partial fill" cannot be the fix, and not for the reason I first gave you. There is no held-back quantity to distribute. The Bin already fills partially, one unit at a time. What fails is the predicate that decides whether the sleeping Rule is told about it.

03Why a sleeping Rule cannot help itself

Because armed and waiting are mutually exclusive. A waiting Rule is not in a Wheel bucket, so Phase 1 never collects it, so Phase 2 never evaluates it. It cannot notice the stock beside it. That is the whole reason the wake predicate is load-bearing rather than an optimisation.

Consequence, and it kills one of the two readings I offered you. The stored shortfall is never honoured. It authorises nothing, guarantees nothing, and is thrown away on wake. It is a filter deciding whether to re-arm — nothing more. So the wait list is not a queue of claims, and I should not have put that to you as a live option when the code had already settled it.

04The working case — the shipped Ruleset, traced

The dwelling kind from rulesets/minimal.toml, with the numbers as authored. Occupancy is 3, because SyntheticCity seats three Households per Building.

  • restock — rate 8, apply {min 1, max 4}, no inputs, outputs +1 sundries. A greedy producer.
  • consume — rate 32, apply {derived = occupancy}, inputs −1 sundries. Draws exactly 3.
  • The sundries Bin has capacity 12.
Trace A — surplus, and it works for ever
t = 96 12 / 12 · headroom 0 restock due → headroom 0 < min 1
subscribes, shortfall = 1
t = 104 12 / 12 · asleep restock not due — it is not on the Wheel at all
t = 128 9 / 12 · headroom 3 consume fires ×3 → Withdraw(3)
drain: 1 ≤ 3 → restock wakes
t = 129 12 / 12 · headroom 0 restock greedy → applies 3 → Deposit(3)
Bin back at its ceiling
t = 136 12 / 12 · headroom 0 restock due → subscribes again, shortfall = 1
the cycle repeats for ever

Nothing trends, both Rules move for ever, and no Rule is ever wrongly asleep. This is the only steady state the project has ever observed.

And now the answer to the question I flagged: it is not luck, but it is not robustness either. restock's shortfall is always exactly 1, because min = 1 and amount = 1, so its deficit is the smallest quantity the engine can express. Any withdrawal at all covers it.

Change min to 2, or the output amount to 2, and a producer fed by withdrawals of 1 sleeps for ever against a Bin with room in it. The shipped Ruleset dodges the defect by having the minimum possible quantum, and the same defect is waiting on the headroom list for anything coarser.

05The broken case — a trickle, which is what pool is

Same Bin, same consumer needing 3. The difference is only the granularity of supply: deliveries arrive one unit at a time, which is exactly what a District Pool draw will look like.

Trace B — the trickle, and the predicate that never passes
t = 0 0 / 12 · needs 3 consume fails → subscribes
shortfall = (3 × 1) − 0 = 3
t = 1 1 / 12 Deposit(1) → drain: 3 > 1 → return
t = 2 2 / 12 Deposit(1) → drain: 3 > 1 → return
t = 3 3 / 12 · satisfiable the Bin now holds exactly what it needs
drain: 3 > 1 → return
t = 12 12 / 12 · full still asleep, shortfall still 3
and now the producer blocks too
t = ∞ 12 / 12 · deadlocked both parties asleep with the Bin full
illegal state

That end state is not merely undesirable — the corpus has already declared it illegal. adr/0033 requires the invariant “no Rule is asleep with all inputs satisfiable” and says the two mitigations for subscription's silent failure mode are both required. 02 §10 lists it in the end-of-run tier. plans/0008 repeats it.

It has never been implemented. A sweep for satisfiab across the tree returns those three statements of intent and one unrelated doc-comment. So the defect and the missing instrument are the same omission seen from two ends — which is why this went undetected through a 100,000-Tick acceptance run.

06The finding: this is two bugs, and the brief filed them as one

The question you were handed — does a wait list wake on the arrival or on the state? — bundles two independent defects. Separating them is what makes the decision tractable.

BugWhat is wrongWhere it showsWhich part of the predicate
1 The budget is this write's delta, not what the Bin holds. A trickle never reaches the threshold, however much accumulates Trace B. Trigger is pool the denominatorremaining = arriving
2 The stored requirement was computed under the Ruleset that was in force when it failed, and nothing re-derives it Halve a Rule's input on a hot reload; the starving Building is the one the edit never reaches the numeratorShortfall[head]

Once they are apart, the candidates stop being two options and become a grid — and the grid shows that the option labelled B on the brief does not fix bug 1 at all. Re-deriving the requirement while still comparing it against a single arrival gives 3 > 1 exactly as before.

07What the level budget costs, honestly

It wakes strictly more waiters than today. Everything it additionally wakes was, by definition, asleep beside satisfiable stock — so every extra wake is a state the corpus calls illegal being corrected. But two costs are real and neither is rhetorical.

An evaluation each

Every wake buys one evaluation next Tick: 82.84 ns synthetic, 552 ns in a real 1M world. Waking a wider prefix is not free, and the Rule engine is already the largest priced consumer in plans/0013.

The wake predicate's promise is not binding

This is the one that touches behaviour. Waking three waiters whose requirements sum to the level does not mean all three are served: Phase 3 applies in a fresh per-Tick shuffle, re-checking atomicity as each is reached, and a greedy waiter can take more than its floor. The losers resubscribe — which is already the designed behaviour, but it means a large waiter loses more often, because it needs more.

And this is where the corpus misled us both. CONTEXT.md → Bin and World.Drain's own remarks justify the narrow budget by saying that waking everybody “would let the sorted settle order pick a permanent winner”. There is no sorted settle order:

_order[i] = Randomness.Draw(_key, IdAt(instance), tick, PurposeTag.RuleSettleOrder)

tick is in the draw, so the order is re-randomised every Tick and no permanent winner is possible. The sentence describes the defect the shuffle was introduced to fix. The worry underneath it survives in a different form — size bias, not identity permanence — which is the fourth time in this corpus that a justification has been false in the letter and true in the substance.

08Why reservations are the wrong exit

The natural instinct — hand each waiter a share as stock arrives — needs a per-waiter committed quantity inside the Bin, because the waiters all draw from one shared Bin and there is nowhere else for an allocation to live. That is worth drawing, because it fails in a way the current defect does not.

Three of ten false or unexercised, and one of them was load-bearing in this very argument. "Never exercised" is worth dwelling on: under local scope, no two Buildings share a Bin, so no wait list in a running world has ever held two waiters. The round-robin has never round-robined. Every fairness property we have been weighing is untested.

What I need from you

One decision, and it is narrower than it was

Reservations are out, partial fill is a non-question, and option A is dominated. What remains is whether the wake predicate is behaviour or bookkeeping:

  • Bookkeeping. Its job is to never leave a Rule asleep beside satisfiable stock, as cheaply as possible. Take the whole grid — derived requirement, level budget — accept the wider wake set, and let Phase 3's shuffle sort out contention. This deletes a saved column, which is the shape adr/0059 rewarded.
  • Behaviour. Its job is also to decide who gets served when supply is short, in which case the narrow budget is a feature, large waiters need protecting from the shuffle, and adr/0033's “the two families differ in who wins a contested resource” applies to the predicate directly.

My recommendation is bookkeeping, and the reason is the last table: the fairness property has never run, its stated justification is false against the code, and the alternative reading requires us to protect a behaviour nobody has ever observed. If fairness is wanted, it should be designed on purpose once pool exists and two Buildings can actually contend — not inferred from a predicate that was written for a different reason.

The counter-argument I would make if you pick behaviour, so you have it: shortage is the regime the entire economy exists to produce (04 §6), the city has never been in it, and choosing the cheap predicate now sets the shape of the first shortage anybody ever sees. That is not nothing.
